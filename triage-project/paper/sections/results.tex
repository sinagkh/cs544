\subsection{RQ1: Accuracy in and out of distribution}
Table~\ref{tab:acc}. In distribution, TF-IDF with logistic regression, trained on 1{,}005 labeled
issues, is the most accurate approach (75.5\%); the few-shot LLM reaches 70.9\% [68.6, 73.3] and
zero-shot 68.5\%. Out of distribution the order reverses. On 2025--26 issues TF-IDF drops to 66.3\%,
while the few-shot LLM reaches 75.3\%. On a new repository TF-IDF and the embedding classifier fall to
60.4\% and 57.5\%; the LLMs lose at most 3 points (68.0\%, 68.4\%). The classical models learn
repository- and period-specific vocabulary; the LLM relies on general knowledge of what bugs, features
and questions look like.

Because the 2025--26 issues postdate the model's training data, they also act as a leakage control:
the LLM is at least as accurate on them as on the 2016--2023 benchmark, so we see no sign that
benchmark contamination inflates its results. The two samples are not directly comparable, though:
label usage itself drifted. React, for example, labeled 200 bugs but only 9 feature requests and 5
questions in 2025--26, as it now routes questions elsewhere.

\subsection{RQ2: Does calibrated deferral keep its promise?}
Raw LLM label probabilities are almost always 0 or 1: temperature scaling needs $T{=}7.9$ (few-shot)
and $T{=}10.3$ (zero-shot) and lowers expected calibration error on the certification issues from
0.23--0.30 to 0.05--0.07. The classical models are underconfident ($T{\approx}0.7$).

\paragraph{Naive thresholds over-promise.} Table~\ref{tab:sel} and Figure~\ref{fig:rc}. A threshold
set where the certification issues show at most 10\% errors gives 10.2--13.3\% errors on the test
set, and under shift up to 20.1\% (TF-IDF, 2025--26) and 27.6\% (TF-IDF, new repository). The lowest
feasible threshold sits where finite-sample noise happens to look favorable.

\paragraph{Rigorous risk control certifies almost nothing at 10\%.} With 330 certification issues,
Learn-then-Test certifies a 10\% threshold only for TF-IDF, which then auto-labels 11.3\% of test
issues with 6 errors in 170 (3.5\%) and 6.8\% of 2025--26 issues with 2 errors in 35. For the embedding
classifier and both LLMs no threshold passes the first test, so every issue goes to a maintainer.
Table~\ref{tab:frontier} shows the trade-off. The few-shot LLM is first certified at a 20\% target,
where it auto-labels 62.9\% of test issues at 19.1\% actual risk and 69.8\% of 2025--26 issues at
17.3\%; the zero-shot LLM needs 25\%. TF-IDF jumps from 17.4\% coverage at a 15\% target to 79.9\% at
20\%. The guarantee does not transfer to a new repository: with the reused calibration the few-shot
LLM's 20\% threshold gives 24.3\% errors there, and LORO calibration on the other repositories
certifies nothing below 25\%. For a new repository the most our data support is that rigorous risk
control is unaffordable at this calibration size; at a 10\% target the reused TF-IDF threshold
auto-labels 3 of 750 issues, too few to judge.

\paragraph{Recalibration helps conformal sets, not thresholds.} Conformal prediction sets cover the
true label in 86.9--88.0\% of test cases (target 90\%). On a new repository with reused calibration,
coverage falls to 75.6\% (TF-IDF) and 83.5\% (embedding); LORO calibration restores it to 87.9\% and
92.0\% (LLMs: 88.1--88.3\%). LORO calibration does not rescue the naive threshold: the few-shot LLM
then auto-labels 19.2\% of new-repository issues at 20.8\% risk, TF-IDF 10.0\% at 24.0\%. An
agreement gate between the LLM and the embedding classifier labels about two thirds of issues at
17.6--26.9\% risk.

\subsection{RQ3: Does the way an issue is written change its label?}
Table~\ref{tab:rob} and Figure~\ref{fig:flips}. TF-IDF is the most fragile to style and structure:
removing template scaffolding or rewriting in non-native English flips 12.5--13.2\% of its labels. The
LLMs are more robust to these, but non-native English still costs the zero-shot LLM 6.9 points of
accuracy and the few-shot LLM 4.4. Removing code is not meaning-preserving in the same sense, since a
stack trace can be the decisive evidence for \emph{bug}; we report it as reduced input. Without code
and in non-native English, the zero-shot LLM loses 10.1 points.

\paragraph{The newcomer sentence acts through the word ``question''.} The full newcomer sentence flips
16.2\% of the few-shot LLM's labels, 14.8\% toward \emph{question}. The controls in
Table~\ref{tab:cues} locate the effect. Relative to a neutral sentence, paired by issue, the phrase
\emph{``sorry if this is a silly question''} alone moves 14.8 points [10.0, 20.0] of issues to
\emph{question}, newcomer status alone 2.9 points [1.0, 5.2], and an apology alone nothing. The
zero-shot LLM shows the same pattern at a smaller scale (6.7 points for the phrase). TF-IDF flips
1.4--7.1\% under every added sentence, the neutral one included, and is not pushed toward
\emph{question}. The LLM reads the reporter's description of their own post as evidence about the
issue's type; newcomer status itself has a small but measurable effect.

\paragraph{Deferral moves the cost to maintainers.} Under the naive threshold, the share of variants
where both versions would be auto-labeled with \emph{different} labels is 0--1.9\% for every
classifier: flips happen among issues the service is already unsure about. The price is review work.
The full newcomer sentence halves the share of the few-shot LLM's auto-labeled issues (33.8\% to
16.2\%), the ``silly question'' phrase alone cuts it to 13.3\%, and non-native rewriting lowers it
from 35.8\% to 32.1\%. Reporters who write this way are labeled automatically less often and wait for
a human more often.

\subsection{RQ4: Are the explanations faithful?}
Asked for a label and two exact quotes as evidence, the few-shot LLM quoted the issue in 178 of 210
cases; 93.1\% of its 331 quotes occur verbatim, and the explained label matches the scored label in
92.9\% of issues. On 164 issues with locatable quotes (on average 130 of 921 body characters), deleting
the quoted evidence lowered the calibrated probability of the predicted label by 0.024, against 0.014
for deleting random text of the same length (Wilcoxon $p{=}0.115$), and changed the label in 6.1\% of
issues against 3.0\%. The evidence is inconclusive: the quotes are real, but we cannot show that they
matter more to the decision than other text of the same length.

\subsection{RQ5: Which signal notices a changed stream?}\label{sec:monitor}
We simulate 300 streams per shift: 400 in-distribution requests, then 200 from 2025--26, from a
repository without history, or in non-native English. A signal alarms when its rate over the last 50
requests exceeds the mean plus three standard deviations of a separate clean calibration stream, drawn
from a disjoint half of the test issues; false alarms are counted on the evaluation stream before the
shift. Table~\ref{tab:mon} and Figure~\ref{fig:mon}: \emph{novelty}, one minus the cosine similarity
to the closest labeled history issue the service may retrieve from, detects a new repository a median
of 13 requests after the shift (all runs) and 2025--26 traffic after 44 (96\% of runs), with under 1\%
false alarms. The model's own signals --- share handed to a human, low confidence --- detect these
shifts in at most 27\% of runs. LLM--classifier disagreement detects a new repository in 63\% of runs
but raises 5--7\% false alarms. No signal reliably notices the non-native stream.

\subsection{Regression tests}
A 40-case suite (issues auto-labeled correctly, wrong predictions that must be handed off, and
non-native rewrites that must keep their label) replays the full pipeline from recorded model scores
in seconds. Injecting plausible bugs and replaying live: removing temperature scaling fails 13 cases
(must-hand-off issues that are then auto-labeled), retrieving examples from other repositories fails
8, and dropping retrieval fails 6. Removing the label definitions from the prompt (2 cases) and
cutting the body to 300 characters (1 case) are barely caught; the suite needs cases aimed at
preprocessing and prompt content.
