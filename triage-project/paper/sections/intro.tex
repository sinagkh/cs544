\section{Introduction}

Open-source projects receive more issues than maintainers can read, and automatic labeling is one of
the most requested forms of help. GitHub now ships an LLM-based labeler as an
Action~\cite{github2025labeler}, and research has moved from fine-tuned classifiers to
LLMs~\cite{colavito2025issue,aracena2025issue}. Benchmarks report how often a labeler is right on a
fixed snapshot~\cite{nlbse24}. A deployed labeler faces different questions. Which labels should it
apply on its own, and which should it leave for a person? Does its promised error rate hold a year
later, or on a repository it has never seen? Does it treat a report differently because the reporter
writes in non-native English or apologizes for being new? And when it explains a decision, is the
explanation what actually drove it?

We study these questions with a triage service built around Qwen2.5-7B, running locally, on the
NLBSE'24 benchmark (3{,}000 issues from five large repositories, 2016--2023) and on issues the same
repositories received in 2025--2026, after the model's training cutoff. We compare the LLM, zero-shot
and with retrieved examples, against classical classifiers trained on the repositories' history, and
ask:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] How do classical and LLM triagers compare in distribution, a year later, and on a new
repository?
\item[RQ2] Can calibrated deferral keep the error among auto-applied labels at a target, and does
that promise survive drift?
\item[RQ3] Do meaning-preserving changes in how an issue is written (no template, a newcomer
sentence, non-native English) change the label, and which part of a newcomer sentence matters?
\item[RQ4] Are the evidence quotes the LLM gives faithful to its decision?
\item[RQ5] Which logged signal notices that the stream of issues has changed?
\end{description}

The answers are mixed in useful ways. A TF-IDF classifier trained on the history is the most accurate
in distribution (75.5\%) but drops to 66.3\% on 2025--26 issues and 60.4\% on a new repository; the
LLM is less accurate in distribution (70.9\%) but stable (75.3\% and 68.0\%). A confidence threshold
tuned to 10\% error on calibration data overshoots in every condition. When thresholds are fitted and
certified on separate data with Learn-then-Test~\cite{angelopoulos2021ltt}, a 10\% target is
certified only for TF-IDF, at 11\% coverage; the few-shot LLM is first certified at 20\% (63\% of issues
auto-labeled), and nothing transfers to a new repository. A sentence in which the reporter calls
their post ``a silly question'' moves the few-shot LLM toward \emph{question} by 14.8 points relative
to a neutral sentence, while saying they are new moves it by 2.9 points. Deferral keeps almost all
such flips from being auto-applied, but halves the share of these reporters' issues that are labeled
automatically. Whether the LLM's evidence quotes drive its decisions remains inconclusive. A simple
novelty signal, distance to the closest labeled history issue, detects a new repository within 13
requests; the model's own confidence rarely notices.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) An evaluation of LLM issue triage as a deployed, selective service,
including a post-cutoff 2025--26 set from the benchmark's own repositories; (2) evidence that common
calibration recipes over-promise, a risk-control frontier showing what a few hundred calibration
issues can certify, and a held-out-repository calibration protocol; (3) controlled rewording tests
that separate the wording of a newcomer sentence from newcomer status, with their effect on
maintainer workload; (4) a faithfulness test of LLM triage explanations; and (5) monitoring and record/replay
regression tests for the service, all on one laptop.
